04Why water reaches the 40th floor

The short version: The pressure in a street main can push water up only a few stories. New York's first gravity aqueduct reached about the sixth floor, so taller buildings learned to pump water to a rooftop tank and let it fall back down.49 Modern towers use booster pumps and split themselves into vertical pressure zones of about 9 to 10 floors, because the main US model plumbing code caps the pressure at a fixture at 80 psi.101126 Fire protection runs on a separate set of pipes, the standpipes, which fire engines can feed from the street through a two-inlet fitting often called a Siamese connection.65

Did you know?Those wooden water tanks on New York rooftops exist because the city's first gravity aqueduct could push water only to about the sixth floor.49

How high can street pressure push water?

Water pressure and height are two ways of measuring the same thing. Each foot of water standing above a point adds about 0.433 pounds per square inch (psi) at that point, and with floors about 10 feet apart, each floor costs about 4.33 psi.101 To lift water one floor, you need about 4.33 psi of push; to deliver it at a usable pressure on that floor, you need more on top.

Plumbing engineers design for a minimum of about 35 psi at the highest fixture in a zone.101 Our arithmetic, using that figure: a street main at 60 psi could in theory lift water about 138 feet (42 m) before friction losses, but to arrive with 35 psi to spare, water can rise only about (60 - 35) / 0.433, or roughly 58 feet. That is five or six floors. Real buildings lose more pressure to pipe friction, meters and backflow preventers, and street pressure varies by neighborhood, so "five or six stories" is a rule of thumb rather than a constant.

History lines up with the arithmetic. New York's Croton Aqueduct, opened in 1842, was a gravity system whose reservoirs sat about 60 feet (18 m) above the city, which was enough to lift water to roughly the sixth story.49

Why do New York buildings have wooden water tanks on the roof?

By the 1880s, New York buildings were rising above 10 stories, past what the Croton system could reach. The answer was a pump that lifted water to a tank on the roof, which then fed the floors below by gravity.49 The city required rooftop tanks on buildings over six stories.49

The tanks are still there, and many are still made of wood:

  • A typical wooden tank is 10 to 12 feet (3 to 3.7 m) high, holds up to 10,000 gallons (about 38 cubic meters), and lasts 25 to 30 years.49
  • An electric pump refills the tank when the level drops, and the bottom of the tank is reserved for emergencies.66
  • Three family firms make about 90% of them: Rosenwach Tank Company, Isseks Brothers and American Pipe and Tank.4966 Isseks Brothers, founded in 1890, was the first firm formed specifically to make tanks.49 Rosenwach builds with Western cedar.66

How many tanks are there? Sources disagree: Vital City, in a 2026 piece by architectural historian Thomas Mellins, estimates more than 5,600, while the real estate site 6sqft says "up to 17,000."4966 The more recent figure is the lower one.

One reason the system has lasted: a gravity tank is simple, needs no power to deliver water, and keeps flowing during a blackout until it runs dry. The pump only has to run when the tank needs refilling.

What is a booster pump, and why are tanks disappearing?

A booster pump raises water pressure so it can reach upper floors. Newer New York buildings increasingly skip the roof tank and use pumps in the basement instead.49 Large modern installations, such as the Burj Khalifa's, use booster sets with variable-speed drives.93 A variable-speed pump speeds up and slows down to hold a steady pressure as demand changes, rather than filling a tank and shutting off.

The trade-off: a pumped system frees the roof, avoids the weight of a full tank, and keeps water from sitting in an open-air vessel, but it depends on power and controls. A tank system has the reverse profile.

What is a pressure zone?

Pumping water to the top of a tall building creates the opposite problem at the bottom: too much pressure. In the US, the International Plumbing Code (IPC), a model code, caps static pressure at fixtures at 80 psi (about 550 kPa) in section 604.8 and requires a pressure-reducing valve (PRV) wherever pressure would exceed that.126

With a minimum of 35 psi at the top fixture and a maximum of 80 psi at the bottom, one zone can span only about 9 to 10 floors.101 Our arithmetic: (80 - 35) / 4.33 psi per floor is about 10 floors. So a tall building is plumbed as a stack of shorter buildings, each with its own pressure range, set by its height, its pumps and its valves.

The design choices that follow:101

  • Separate booster pumps for each zone are the most efficient approach up to about 40 to 60 stories.
  • Ultra-high-rises add intermediate tanks partway up. Pumps lift water to a tank on a mechanical floor, and a second set of pumps draws from that tank (a "suction tank") to feed the zones above.
  • Water heaters are best placed inside the zone they serve, because PRVs complicate the loops that keep hot water circulating to distant fixtures.

This is one reason many towers have mechanical floors partway up, often with louvers instead of windows. Some of that space can house the pumps and tanks for the zones above.

How does the Burj Khalifa get water to the top?

The world's tallest building as of 2026, in Dubai, uses the staged approach at full scale. Xylem supplied six water transfer pump sets and seven pressure booster sets, all with variable-speed drives.93 The pumps sit in the basement and on two technical floors about a third of the way up.93 Average supply is 946,000 liters (about 250,000 gallons) a day, supplemented by water recovered from air-conditioning condensate, and about 1,000 cubic meters a day are boosted all the way to the top.93

How do firefighters get water up a tall building?

Domestic plumbing and fire protection are separate systems with separate rules. A standpipe is a vertical pipe that feeds hose valves inside a larger building.65 It lets firefighters connect hoses on the floor near the fire instead of dragging them up from the street.

On the outside of the building, at street level, is the fire department connection, often called a Siamese connection: a fitting with at least two inlets.65 Fire engines pump water through the Siamese into the building's standpipes and sprinkler system to raise flow and pressure.65 The US standard NFPA 14 requires a sign at each connection saying which system it feeds.65

Pressure limits apply here too. NFPA 14 caps pressure at standpipe hose connections at 175 psi (about 1,200 kPa).126 The same zoning logic that governs sinks and showers applies to fire hoses, at a higher ceiling, because hose crews need usable pressure without dangerous excess on the lower floors.

Standpipes come in two broad kinds. Wet standpipes keep water in the pipe at all times; automatic wet systems rely on a fire pump for pressure, while manual wet systems have no pump behind them.127 Dry standpipes hold air instead, and in a manual dry system, with no pump or water supply of its own, firefighters must charge the pipe through the fire department connection before they can attack the fire.127 The fire pumps themselves are covered in the US by a separate NFPA standard, NFPA 20, the Standard for the Installation of Stationary Pumps for Fire Protection.128

In Boston

Greater Boston's regional supply arrives from the Quabbin and Wachusett reservoirs through deep gravity tunnels, and the local water departments distribute it from there.10867 (The full route is in Where your tap water comes from.) The physics in Boston is the same as in New York or Dubai: street pressure serves the lower floors, and anything tall pumps.

Our arithmetic, if a pressure cap like the IPC's 80 psi applies: a 40-story Boston tower would need about four pressure zones, and anything much taller would face the same choice New York and Dubai engineers face between per-zone boosters and intermediate tanks.101 The IPC is a model code, and Massachusetts writes its own.

Massachusetts does set its own cap at the same number. The state plumbing code, 248 CMR 10.14(2)(e), requires a pressure-reducing valve wherever pressure at a fixture exceeds 80 psi, and section 10.14(2)(d) requires a booster pump and pressure tank, or another compliant means, where supply pressure cannot meet the code's minimums.129 On the street side, a 2013 BWSC planning presentation described Boston's distribution system as five pressure zones with pressures from 60 to 112 psi.130 We did not find a source on whether rooftop tanks or basement boosters dominate in Boston's towers.

How it changed

  • 1842: New York's Croton Aqueduct opens, delivering water by gravity to about the sixth story.49
  • 1880s: Buildings pass 10 stories, and pump-filled rooftop tanks become standard.49
  • 1890: Isseks Brothers is founded, the first company formed specifically to make New York's rooftop tanks.49
  • 20th century to now: Newer buildings increasingly use basement booster pumps instead of roof tanks.49 Supertall towers stack pumps and tanks on intermediate floors, as the Burj Khalifa does.93

In our reading, the history is a shift from storage to control. The roof tank stored water at the right height and let gravity do the work. Variable-speed pumps hold pressure electronically, floor by floor, and depend on power and controls to do it.

What's changing now (2026)

  • New York's tanks are still being built. Writing in May 2026, Vital City describes wooden tanks as a living trade, with three family firms making most of them, even as newer buildings shift to basement pumps.49
  • Booster design keeps evolving. Engineering guidance published in November 2025 covers booster pump design for high-rises within the 80 psi fixture cap and 175 psi standpipe cap.126
  • Pumps run more of the system. As more towers use pumps instead of gravity tanks, backup power matters more for keeping water flowing to upper floors during an outage (see How electricity gets to your building).
